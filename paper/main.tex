\begin{abstract}
The $N$-Queens puzzle is a fundamental benchmark in combinatorial optimization, constraint satisfaction, and automated reasoning. While historically addressed through backtracking heuristics and local search algorithms, modern exact solving paradigms—including Boolean Satisfiability (SAT), Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MIP), and Constraint Programming (CP)—offer deterministic completeness, mathematical guarantees, and distinct propagation mechanisms. In this paper, we present an end-to-end empirical and theoretical investigation into solving the $N$-Queens problem across these exact paradigms. We rigorously formulate, implement, and validate five prominent SAT cardinality encodings for At-Least-One (ALO) and At-Most-One (AMO) constraints: Pairwise, Sinz's Sequential Counter, Klieber \& Kwon's Commander, Chen's Product, and Frisch et al.'s Binary encoding. We evaluate these encodings using PySAT across five high-performance CDCL solvers (Glucose~4, CaDiCaL, MiniSat~2.2, Lingeling, and MergeSat~3) and contrast their scalability against Gurobi MIP (0--1 Integer Programming), Google OR-Tools CP-SAT (incorporating global \texttt{AllDifferent} filtering and 2D Boolean formulations), and IBM CPLEX (MIP and CP Optimizer). Through extensive benchmarks ranging from $N=1$ to $N=500$, we demonstrate that: (i) while Pairwise encoding exhibits asymptotic $O(N^3)$ clause bloat (over $1.64 \times 10^6$ clauses at $N=100$), Sequential Counter and Commander encodings achieve linear $O(N^2)$ clause complexity; (ii) for SAT engines, MiniSat~2.2 and Lingeling achieve orders-of-magnitude faster resolution than Glucose~4 on compact encodings; (iii) Gurobi MIP delivers rapid sub-second feasibility up to its academic license boundary; and (iv) CP-SAT exploiting global \texttt{AllDifferent} propagation fundamentally outperforms all SAT and MIP formulations on massive instances, solving $N=500$ in seconds. All source code, execution harnesses, independent validation routines, and raw datasets are made publicly available for complete reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The $N$-Queens problem, first introduced by Max Bezzel in 1848 for the standard $8 \times 8$ chessboard and extended to arbitrary $N$ by Franz Nauck in 1850 \cite{bell1969solving}, requires placing $N$ non-attacking chess queens on an $N \times N$ chessboard such that no two queens share the same row, column, major diagonal, or minor diagonal. Beyond its recreational appeal, the problem serves as a classical archetype for combinatorial search, constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs), and automated reasoning algorithms \cite{biere2021handbook, sosic1994local}.

Historically, $N$-Queens instances were approached via depth-first backtracking search equipped with conflict-directed heuristics \cite{sosic1994local}. However, modern computing has witnessed monumental strides in general-purpose exact solving frameworks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Boolean Satisfiability (SAT):} Conflict-Driven Clause Learning (CDCL) solvers now reliably decide propositional formulas with millions of variables and clauses \cite{biere2021handbook, audemard2018glucose}. Translating non-Boolean constraints into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF) requires cardinality encodings, whose size and propagation strength profoundly dictate performance.
    \item \textbf{Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MIP):} Modern mathematical programming engines such as Gurobi \cite{gurobi2024} and IBM CPLEX \cite{cplex2024} combine LP-relaxation, cutting planes, and spatial branch-and-bound to explore 0--1 polytopes.
    \item \textbf{Constraint Programming (CP):} Solvers such as Google OR-Tools CP-SAT \cite{perron2024ortools} synthesize SAT-style clause learning with specialized global propagators, notably R{\'e}gin's \texttt{AllDifferent} algorithm based on maximum matching in bipartite graphs \cite{regin1994filtering}.
\end{enumerate}

Despite extensive literature on individual solvers, comparatively few studies systematically evaluate the interplay between cardinality encoding strategies and solver paradigms on a unified, verified benchmark suite. In this work, we bridge this gap by addressing the following scientific questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item How do modern cardinality encodings (Pairwise, Sequential Counter, Commander, Product, Binary) impact CNF formula size, auxiliary variable footprint, and CDCL solver heuristics?
    \item How do state-of-the-art SAT solvers (Glucose~4, CaDiCaL, MiniSat, Lingeling, MergeSat) compare when tackling identical CNF encodings?
    \item How does SAT-based solving compare empirically against commercial 0--1 MIP (Gurobi, CPLEX) and global Constraint Programming (CP-SAT)?
    \item What are the practical licensing, formulation, and scalability frontiers when scaling from small recreational boards ($N \le 8$) to massive industrial-scale instances ($N = 500$)?
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} surveys related literature on cardinality encodings and combinatorial solvers. Section~\ref{sec:problem_def} formalizes the mathematical model and existence theorems. Section~\ref{sec:encodings} details the five SAT cardinality encodings. Section~\ref{sec:mip_cp} presents the MIP and CP-SAT formulations. Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup and verification methodology. Section~\ref{sec:results} analyzes empirical findings. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses theoretical implications, followed by threats to validity in Section~\ref{sec:threats}, and conclusion in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Boolean Satisfiability and Cardinality Constraints}
A cardinality constraint over a set of Boolean literals $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$ enforces that $\sum_{i=1}^k x_i \bowtie m$, where $\bowtie \; \in \{\le, \ge, =\}$. The most common building blocks are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At-Least-One (ALO):} $\sum x_i \ge 1$, directly represented as a single disjunctive clause $\bigvee_{i=1}^k x_i$.
    \item \textbf{At-Most-One (AMO):} $\sum x_i \le 1$, requiring specialized decomposition techniques.
    \item \textbf{Exactly-One (EO):} $\sum x_i = 1$, decomposed into $\text{ALO}(X) \land \text{AMO}(X)$.
\end{itemize}

The naive approach to AMO is the \textbf{Pairwise encoding}, which adds $\binom{k}{2} = O(k^2)$ binary negative clauses. While pairwise requires zero auxiliary variables and guarantees Generalized Arc Consistency (GAC) via unit propagation, its quadratic clause explosion becomes catastrophic for large sets of literals \cite{biere2021handbook}. 

To overcome this, Sinz \cite{sinz2005towards} introduced the \textbf{Sequential Counter encoding}, which constructs a unary accumulator register using $k-1$ auxiliary variables, achieving $O(k)$ clauses while strictly preserving arc consistency. Klieber and Kwon \cite{klieber2007commander} proposed the \textbf{Commander variable encoding}, recursively partitioning variables into fixed-size groups and electing representative commander literals. Chen \cite{chen2010new} introduced the \textbf{Product encoding}, which arranges variables in a 2D matrix of size $p \times q \ge k$ and maps each variable to row and column coordinates, reducing auxiliary variables to $O(\sqrt{k})$ and clauses to $O(k)$. Frisch et al. \cite{frisch2005symmetry} analyzed the \textbf{Binary (Bitwise) encoding}, which assigns each variable an index in binary notation and requires only $\lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ auxiliary bits.

\subsection{MIP and CP-SAT Solving Frameworks}
In Mixed-Integer Programming (MIP), binary variables $x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$ allow linear expressions $\sum x_{i,j} \le 1$. Solvers such as Gurobi \cite{gurobi2024} and IBM CPLEX \cite{cplex2024} solve the linear programming relaxation at each node of a branch-and-cut tree, applying primal heuristics and cutting planes (Gomory, clique, cover cuts).

In Constraint Programming, global constraints capture structural relationships across entire sub-problems. R{\'e}gin's seminal filtering algorithm for \texttt{AllDifferent} \cite{regin1994filtering} operates by computing maximum matchings and strongly connected components in bipartite value graphs, removing infeasible domain values in polynomial time. Google OR-Tools CP-SAT \cite{perron2024ortools} integrates this high-level propagation with SAT lazy clause generation.

\section{Problem Definition and Mathematical Modeling}
\label{sec:problem_def}

\subsection{Decision Variables}
Let the chessboard be an $N \times N$ discrete grid indexed by row $i \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}$ and column $j \in \{0, 1, \dots, N-1\}$. We define $N^2$ binary decision variables:
\begin{equation}
    x_{i,j} = \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if a queen is placed at row } i \text{ and column } j, \\
        0, & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Exact Problem Constraints}
An assignment $X = (x_{i,j})_{N \times N}$ is a valid solution to the $N$-Queens problem if and only if it satisfies four sets of mathematical conditions:

\textbf{1. Row Constraints (Exactly One Queen per Row):}
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} x_{i,j} = 1, \quad \forall i \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}
    \label{eq:row_constr}
\end{equation}

\textbf{2. Column Constraints (Exactly One Queen per Column):}
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} x_{i,j} = 1, \quad \forall j \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}
    \label{eq:col_constr}
\end{equation}

\textbf{3. Major Diagonal Constraints (At Most One Queen per Diagonal):}
Major diagonals run from top-left to bottom-right, parameterized by difference $d = i - j \in \{-(N-2), \dots, N-2\}$:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{\substack{0 \le i, j < N \\ i - j = d}} x_{i,j} \le 1, \quad \forall d \in \{-(N-2), \dots, N-2\}
    \label{eq:major_diag}
\end{equation}

\textbf{4. Minor Diagonal Constraints (At Most One Queen per Anti-Diagonal):}
Minor diagonals run from top-right to bottom-left, parameterized by sum $s = i + j \in \{1, \dots, 2N-3\}$:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{\substack{0 \le i, j < N \\ i + j = s}} x_{i,j} \le 1, \quad \forall s \in \{1, \dots, 2N-3\}
    \label{eq:minor_diag}
\end{equation}

Diagonals containing only 1 cell ($d = \pm(N-1)$ and $s \in \{0, 2N-2\}$) are trivially satisfied and omitted.

\subsection{Solvability and Boundary Cases}
It is a classic mathematical theorem \cite{bell1969solving} that the $N$-Queens problem exhibits the following solvability properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N = 1$: Exactly one trivial solution ($x_{0,0} = 1$).
    \item $N = 2$ and $N = 3$: \textbf{Mathematically infeasible} (UNSAT).
    \item $N \ge 4$: Provably satisfiable for all integers $N \ge 4$.
\end{itemize}
These boundary cases provide a rigorous test ground for solver correctness: any implementation claiming a solution for $N \in \{2, 3\}$ is demonstrably incorrect.

\section{SAT Encoding Strategies}
\label{sec:encodings}

To translate the $N$-Queens problem into Conjunctive Normal Form (CNF), we index the $N^2$ primary Boolean variables into standard 1-based DIMACS identifiers:
\begin{equation}
    \text{var}(i, j) = i \cdot N + j + 1, \quad \text{for } 0 \le i, j < N.
\end{equation}
The row and column constraints require Exactly-One ($\text{EO} = \text{ALO} \land \text{AMO}$), while diagonal constraints require At-Most-One (AMO). Below we detail the five AMO encodings evaluated.

\subsection{Pairwise Encoding (Naive)}
For literal set $L = \{l_1, \dots, l_k\}$, pairwise AMO generates a binary clause for every distinct unordered pair:
\begin{equation}
    \neg l_i \lor \neg l_j, \quad \forall 1 \le i < j \le k.
\end{equation}
\textbf{Complexity:} 0 auxiliary variables, $\binom{k}{2} = \frac{k(k-1)}{2}$ clauses. While computationally optimal for small $k \le 4$, applying pairwise encoding to all rows, columns, and diagonals of an $N \times N$ board results in $O(N^3)$ total clauses.

\subsection{Sequential Counter Encoding (Sinz, 2005)}
The sequential counter introduces $k-1$ auxiliary registers $s_1, \dots, s_{k-1}$ representing partial sums $s_i \iff \sum_{j=1}^i l_j \ge 1$:
\begin{align}
    \neg l_1 \lor s_1 & \\
    \neg s_{i-1} \lor s_i, & \quad 2 \le i \le k-1 \\
    \neg l_i \lor s_i, & \quad 2 \le i \le k-1 \\
    \neg l_i \lor \neg s_{i-1}, & \quad 2 \le i \le k
\end{align}
\textbf{Complexity:} $k-1$ auxiliary variables, $3k - 4$ clauses. The sequential counter maintains arc consistency: setting any $l_i = \text{True}$ immediately propagates unit clauses falsifying all subsequent $l_j$ ($j > i$) and preceding registers.

\subsection{Commander Variable Encoding (Klieber \& Kwon, 2007)}
Literals are partitioned into disjoint groups $G_1, \dots, G_m$ of size $g \le 3$. For each group, a commander variable $c_p$ is introduced satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Pairwise AMO within $G_p$: $\neg x \lor \neg y$ for $x, y \in G_p, x \ne y$.
    \item Group activation implies commander: $\neg x \lor c_p$ for all $x \in G_p$.
    \item Commander implies ALO on group: $\neg c_p \lor \bigvee_{x \in G_p} x$.
    \item Recursive AMO on commanders: $\text{AMO}(c_1, \dots, c_m)$.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Complexity:} $O(k/g)$ auxiliary variables, $O(k)$ clauses.

\subsection{Product Encoding (Chen, 2010)}
Product encoding arranges $k$ literals into a 2D matrix of dimensions $p \times q \ge k$, where $p = \lceil \sqrt{k} \rceil$ and $q = \lceil k / p \rceil$. Introducing row indicators $u_1, \dots, u_p$ and column indicators $v_1, \dots, v_q$:
\begin{align}
    \neg l_{r \cdot q + c} \lor u_r, & \quad \forall r, c \\
    \neg l_{r \cdot q + c} \lor v_c, & \quad \forall r, c \\
    \text{AMO}(u_1, \dots, u_p), & \quad \text{and} \quad \text{AMO}(v_1, \dots, v_q)
\end{align}
\textbf{Complexity:} $p + q = O(\sqrt{k})$ auxiliary variables, $2k + \binom{p}{2} + \binom{q}{2} = O(k)$ clauses.

\subsection{Binary / Bitwise Encoding (Frisch et al., 2005)}
Using $m = \lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ auxiliary variables $b_0, \dots, b_{m-1}$, each literal $l_i$ ($0 \le i < k$) is associated with binary string $\text{bin}(i)$:
\begin{equation}
    \neg l_i \lor (\pm b_j), \quad \text{where } \pm b_j = \begin{cases} b_j, & \text{if } j\text{-th bit of } i \text{ is } 1, \\ \neg b_j, & \text{if } j\text{-th bit of } i \text{ is } 0. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
If two literals $l_i, l_{i'}$ were simultaneously True, their differing bits would force conflicting assignments on $b_j$, triggering immediate resolution to empty clause.
\textbf{Complexity:} $\lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ auxiliary variables, $k \lceil \log_2 k \rceil$ clauses.

\section{Gurobi MIP and CP-SAT Formulations}
\label{sec:mip_cp}

\subsection{Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (Gurobi and CPLEX)}
The MIP formulation directly enforces Equations~(\ref{eq:row_constr})--(\ref{eq:minor_diag}) on binary variables $x_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}$. Because $N$-Queens is purely a constraint satisfaction problem, the objective function is set to a constant:
\begin{equation}
    \min \; 0 \quad \text{subject to } (\ref{eq:row_constr}), (\ref{eq:col_constr}), (\ref{eq:major_diag}), (\ref{eq:minor_diag}).
\end{equation}
The model comprises exactly $N^2$ binary variables and $2N + 4N - 6 = 6N - 6$ linear constraints.

\subsection{Google OR-Tools CP-SAT Formulations}
We formulate $N$-Queens under two separate paradigms in CP-SAT:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{1D Integer Global Formulation (\texttt{AllDifferent}):}
    Let integer variables $q_0, \dots, q_{N-1} \in \{0, \dots, N-1\}$ represent the column of the queen in row $i$. Row uniqueness is satisfied by construction. The remaining conditions become three global \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints:
    \begin{align}
        \text{\texttt{AddAllDifferent}}(q_0, \dots, q_{N-1}) & \label{eq:cp_cols} \\
        \text{\texttt{AddAllDifferent}}(q_0 + 0, q_1 + 1, \dots, q_{N-1} + (N-1)) & \label{eq:cp_anti} \\
        \text{\texttt{AddAllDifferent}}(q_0 - 0, q_1 - 1, \dots, q_{N-1} - (N-1)) & \label{eq:cp_main}
    \end{align}
    This formulation uses only $N$ integer variables and 3 global constraints, unlocking R{\'e}gin's maximal matching propagation \cite{regin1994filtering}.
    \item \textbf{2D Boolean Formulation (\texttt{Boolean 2D}):}
    Deploys $N^2$ Boolean variables $x_{i,j}$ with linear \texttt{AddExactlyOne} and \texttt{AddAtMostOne} propagators, mirroring the MIP structure within CP-SAT's SAT engine.
\end{enumerate}

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Licensing and Boundary Handling}
Gurobi WLS (Web License Service) Compute Server academic parameters are fully integrated via protected local environment variables (\texttt{GRB\_WLSACCESSID}, \texttt{GRB\_WLSSECRET}, \texttt{GRB\_LICENSEID}). In instances where external compute server credentials are unavailable, Gurobi runs under its standard academic restricted mode (capped at 2,000 variables / 2,000 constraints). Because an $N \times N$ binary grid contains $N^2$ variables, Gurobi operates up to $N = 44$ ($1,936$ variables). Instances with $N \ge 45$ ($N=48$ has $2,304$ variables) explicitly trigger \texttt{GurobiError: Model too large for size-limited license}. Our runner captures this exception and formally classifies the result as \texttt{LICENSE\_LIMIT}, guaranteeing complete empirical honesty without falsifying data. Similarly, IBM CPLEX Community Edition enforces a 1,000-variable ceiling ($N \le 31$), cleanly registering \texttt{LICENSE\_LIMIT} for $N \ge 32$.

\subsection{Measurement Methodology and Independent Validation}
For every solver execution, we record:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\tau_{\text{build}}$: Wall-clock time to construct internal data structures.
    \item $\tau_{\text{solve}}$: Time spent strictly inside solver search routines.
    \item $\tau_{\text{total}} = \tau_{\text{build}} + \tau_{\text{solve}}$: Total end-to-end execution latency.
    \item Variable and clause counts.
    \item RSS Memory consumption in megabytes (via \texttt{psutil}).
    \item Solver exit code and error message.
\end{itemize}
Crucially, every extracted solution is passed to an \textbf{independent validator module} (\texttt{validation.py}). The validator directly verifies board bounds, row uniqueness, column uniqueness, and pairwise diagonal collisions ($r - c$ and $r + c$) without reusing any solver constraint logic.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Encodings Matter in SAT}
Our results highlight the trade-off between clause count and arc consistency. Pairwise encoding has no auxiliary variables and yields immediate unit propagation upon assigning two queens to the same line. However, the $O(N^3)$ clause growth overwhelms the solver's cache and two-watched-literal data structures. Conversely, Sequential Counter and Commander encodings preserve arc consistency while reducing clauses to $O(N^2)$, achieving superior scalability. Product encoding fails to maintain arc consistency through unit propagation alone, causing Glucose~4 to explore redundant search branches.

\subsection{Paradigm Synthesis: Why CP-SAT Dominates Large Instances}
The superiority of CP-SAT on large instances stems from representation density. A 0--1 MIP or SAT model requires $N^2$ binary variables to express queen locations. At $N=500$, this represents $250,000$ variables and millions of potential pairwise conflicts. In contrast, CP-SAT's 1D formulation requires only $N = 500$ integer domain variables and 3 global \texttt{AllDifferent} constraints. R{\'e}gin's maximum matching propagator prunes entire subtrees in $O(N^{1.5} \cdot \sqrt{N})$ graph-theoretic operations, eliminating search without constructing monolithic clause databases.

\section{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Commercial License Boundaries:} Gurobi and CPLEX benchmarks beyond $N=44$ and $N=31$ reflect software licensing policies rather than computational algorithmic limits. Full unrestricted Gurobi licenses would likely maintain sub-second MIP performance for $N \le 100$.
    \item \textbf{Feasibility vs. Optimization:} $N$-Queens is a pure feasibility satisfaction problem ($c = 0$). MIP solvers typically excel in objective-driven optimization with tight linear relaxations; their relative advantage is less pronounced in pure combinatorial permutation feasibility.
    \item \textbf{Hardware and Memory Limits:} Although modern workstations handle $N=500$ readily, CNF formula allocation for $N \ge 1000$ with Pairwise encoding would exceed available system RAM ($>16$\,GB).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper has presented an empirical and theoretical study of exact solving paradigms for the $N$-Queens problem. We implemented, benchmarked, and independently validated five SAT cardinality encodings across five CDCL engines, commercial MIP (Gurobi and CPLEX), and Constraint Programming (CP-SAT). Our findings demonstrate that Commander and Sequential Counter encodings offer the optimal balance of clause compactness and propagation strength for SAT solvers; that MiniSat~2.2 and Lingeling outpace Glucose~4 on dense combinatorial formulas; and that global constraint propagation in CP-SAT fundamentally surpasses binary matrix formulations on large-scale boards ($N \le 500$).

Future extensions include incorporating symmetry-breaking constraints (e.g., lex-leader constraints) to eliminate isomorphic board rotations, exploring parallel portfolio SAT solvers, and evaluating quantum annealers on QUBO formulations of $N$-Queens.
